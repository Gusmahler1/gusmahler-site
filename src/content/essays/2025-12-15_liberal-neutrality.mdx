## Introduction: Why Neutrality Matters



In the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, the concept of liberal neutrality — most commonly understood as the idea that government must remain neutral among competing conceptions of the good — came to wield extraordinary influence in American political culture. Even as scholars continued to debate whether genuine neutrality was even possible in a polarized and morally fractured society, public figures across institutions increasingly presented their judgments as emerging from a neutral, unbiased standpoint. A characteristic of liberalism had become a free-standing ideal, capable of justifying political or institutional authority on its own. Abiding by procedural neutrality became a prerequisite for undertaking any substantive political action. The contemporary aspiration toward neutrality has shaped how authority is justified across a wide range of institutional domains in the United States. For instance, though the Supreme Court was designed to be an impartial arbiter of the Constitution, in recent decades the Court has made a point of explicitly emphasizing its commitment to neutrality — even as public confidence in its impartiality wanes. The relatively recent ascent of originalism — the generally conservative jurisprudential approach that grounds interpretation in the Constitution’s original public understanding — was fueled in large part by its success in framing substantive interpretive commitments as the logical outcomes of objective historical analysis. In 2005, the ideal of judicial neutrality was articulated in especially vivid terms during Chief Justice John Roberts’s confirmation hearings, where he famously likened judges to



umpires, insisting that “it’s my job to call balls and strikes, and not to pitch or bat.”1 A parallel commitment to neutrality emerged in journalism: Leonard Downie Jr., who ran The Washington Post from 1991 until 2008, refused to vote during his tenure in order to avoid even the appearance of partiality, explaining that he did not want to form personal opinions about the issues he covered professionally. Though these pledges to neutrality are themselves isolated statements, taken together, they reflect a broader cultural shift in which political legitimacy increasingly came to be associated with the posture of neutrality. In turn, authority was thought to rest on the ability of decision-makers to present themselves as impartial arbiters rather than as advocates of contested moral visions. This emphasis on neutrality extended even to the most morally charged debates in the United States. When neutrality qua neutrality was first rising to prominence and gaining political appeal in the 1970s, it saw primitive expression in the Supreme Court’s reasoning in Roe v. Wade (1973). Justice Blackmun grounded abortion rights in individual liberty, maintaining that the Court “need not resolve the difficult question of when life begins,” since experts in “medicine, philosophy, and theology are unable to arrive at any consensus.”3 In light of this moral uncertainty and the personal and social burdens borne by pregnant women, the Court concluded that the decision should rest with the individual rather than the state. This refusal to adjudicate between competing moral claims reflects the procedural liberal impulse to protect individual liberty without endorsing any particular conception of the good.

As political philosopher Michael Sandel argues, liberalism’s “penchant for neutrality” pushes it toward market arrangements because markets appear to respect individual autonomy by allowing people to pursue their own ends without state interference. This commitment to neutrality has been invoked not only to defend the primacy of markets but also to question redistributive welfare policies. Milton Friedman, for instance, argued that progressive taxation violates individual freedom because it uses coercion to impose the state’s conception of an equitable society on the wealthy. In this context, state neutrality is understood as procedural restraint, requiring minimal government interference in economic choices regardless of the distributive inequalities that result. In recent years, however, the desirability — and even the practical possibility — of such neutrality has come under increasing scrutiny. Critics argue that political decisions about abortion, economic distribution, or the structure of markets cannot truly avoid moral judgements. Sandel contends that attempts to exclude moral reasoning from public debate do not produce neutrality at all; instead, they allow markets and those who dominate them to decide fundamental questions by default: “This [effort to remove moral questions from political calculations] is, in the end, a false promise. Banishing morally contested questions from public debate does not leave them undecided; it simply means that markets, superintended by the wealthy and the powerful, will decide these questions for us.”11

Others have made similar critiques of neutrality when important moral questions are at stake. For these skeptics, cases like Roe and Casey cannot be resolved without appealing to comprehensive moral worldviews, revealing the limits of neutrality in constitutional adjudication. Sandel forcefully advances this critique, arguing that attempts to justify abortion rights on grounds of state neutrality inevitably smuggle in substantive moral judgments. Proponents of abortion rights often contend that because the moral status of the fetus is deeply contested, the state should refrain from imposing any particular view on when a life begins. Yet, as Sandel points out, this posture is itself morally loaded. Permitting abortion on demand implicitly denies fetal personhood and, correspondingly, violates the moral convictions held by many. Furthermore, if fetuses are in fact full-fledged individuals, then a legal regime justified in the name of neutrality would in fact be gravely unjust. Sandel’s critique thus underscores a broader claim shared by critics of liberal neutrality — that pure neutrality is impossible to sustain in any domain where fundamental rights, values, and conceptions of the good are at stake. So, given these practical and philosophical difficulties, how did neutrality come to be a central tenet of contemporary liberalism? There is no single answer to this question, but many scholars trace the modern articulation of liberal neutrality to the work of American philosopher John Rawls, most notably his seminal 1971 book A Theory of Justice. Rawls sought to identify principles of justice that could regulate the basic structure of society by appealing to fairness rather than to any particular moral or religious doctrine. He named this project justice as fairness, and grounded it in the idea of the original position — a hypothetical standpoint from which free and equal persons choose principles of justice without knowledge of their own position in society



or any preconceived conception of the good. Although Rawls did not explicitly frame his project in terms of neutrality, his effort to derive political principles from this impartial perspective helped lay the groundwork for neutrality’s elevation into a central liberal ideal. In the decades after A Theory of Justice was published, Rawls continued to refine justice as fairness while keeping its core commitments to liberty and equality intact. A Theory of Justice would remain his most influential work, with numerous public figures invoking the concept of neutrality that he popularized in order to justify their political positions. And yet, in many cases, they would misrepresent his theories by failing to account for the qualifications and limitations that Rawls himself stressed. In the process, the public perception of Rawls’ theories came to diverge from his actual theories. This divergence was a testament to the malleability and political utility of neutrality as a governing principle. Rawls had set in motion a political theory that would shape American politics even as it took on a life of its own. In a pluralistic democracy like ours, where citizens hold divergent moral, religious, and philosophical commitments, it makes sense that appeals to neutrality would carry intuitive force, promising a way to govern amid disagreement without restoring to coercion. It may even be true that some measure of neutrality is indispensable to liberal democracy. This paper argues, however, that the Rawlsian conception of neutrality devised a half-century ago, was stretched far beyond its intended purpose. While a limited form of neutrality long functioned as a background safeguard for pluralism in American political life, the elevation of neutrality into a powerful, affirmative principle of justification transformed it into something far less neutral. Today, appeals to neutrality are more often deployed to defend



contested socioeconomic arrangements than to serve neutrality’s properly conceived purpose: ensuring that citizens are treated as free and equal under the law. To understand how this transformation occurred, I trace the evolution of neutrality from its primitive forms, through Rawls’s theory of justice, to its later neoliberal appropriation. In doing so, I distinguish Rawlsian neutrality from the procedural foil that dominates contemporary discourse and is frequently invoked to contest any attempts to promote shared societal ends. Ultimately, I claim that criticisms leveled against “liberal neutrality” today in fact target this subsequent, distorted understanding of neutrality rather than Rawls’s original conception.



## Early Forms of Neutrality



Contemporary liberal neutrality holds that the state should not privilege any particular conception of the good life. This commitment flows from liberalism’s foundational belief that individuals are free and equal moral agents, each entitled to form and pursue their own understanding of what gives life meaning. Once this premise is accepted, it logically follows that political authority must be constrained so that it does not impose contested moral ends on citizens. In the context of a pluralistic democracy, state neutrality thus presents a fairly intuitive solution to the problem of governing amid deep moral disagreement. It’s important to note, though, that the liberal assumption that political authority must honor individuals’ moral independence and refrain from cultivating their virtues or advancing any shared substantive vision of the good is far from self-evident. For most of human history, political thinkers took the opposite view — that the state could and should promote substantive



conceptions of the good and protect the moral vitality of the community, even when doing so constrained individual freedom. Liberalism reversed this ordering. Liberalism’s recentering of the individual instead of the community as the primary political actor did not occur in a vacuum. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, growing religious pluralism in Europe made republican models of governance based on shared conceptions of the good life increasingly difficult to sustain. If people fundamentally disagreed about what constitutes human flourishing — about which religious truths matter, which virtues should be cultivated and which ends are worth pursuing — how could the state legitimately promote a distinct vision of the good? More fundamentally, on what basis could it claim the authority to do so at all? These questions motivated early liberals to rethink the nature and scope of political authority. Political philosophers such as John Locke argued that individuals possess natural rights prior to political society and that governments exist to secure these rights, not to tell citizens how to live. Locke himself was deeply religious, yet he nonetheless maintained that civil authority had no rightful jurisdiction over matters of faith. This shift from paternalistic authority to rights-protecting government laid the groundwork for a limited form of neutrality concerned with restraining coercive power and ensuring equal civil standing. Drawing directly on Lockean principles of religious toleration and limited government, the American founders enshrined protections of speech and religion in the Constitution to prevent certain beliefs from being persecuted — a far narrower commitment to neutrality than that commonly held today. In the early American context, neutrality was taken to require



impartial treatment of citizens who held differing beliefs, not state abstention from judgments about the good altogether. Religious liberty, in particular, was framed in these terms. While the new republic rejected sectarian establishment and coercion of conscience, it did not regard religion itself as morally neutral or politically irrelevant. On the contrary, leading figures such as George Washington openly affirmed its civic value: Of all the dispositions and habits which lead to political prosperity, religion and morality are indispensable supports. In vain would that man claim the tribute of patriotism who should labor to subvert these great pillars of human happiness, these firmest props of the duties of men and citizens. The mere politician, equally with the pious man, ought to respect and to cherish them. A volume could not trace all their connections with private and public felicity. With respect to religion, neutrality thus meant that the state could not privilege one denomination over another, not that it was required to suspend judgment about the social and moral importance of religion itself. The aim was to foster tolerance amid pluralism. This limited conception of neutrality — concerned primarily with non-persecution and equal standing — prevailed for many generations, showing that while America’s political framework has always possessed a philosophical architecture that can accommodate neutrality, it was not always defined by its commitment to neutrality. Throughout American history, various philosophical and political frameworks — including utilitarianism and Keynesianism, which I discuss shortly — have embodied certain forms and elements of neutrality without making it foundational. What has changed is the weight neutrality carries and the scope of what it demands. In the twentieth century, neutrality moved from a secondary constraint on political



authority to a central criterion of legitimacy, increasingly understood as requiring the state to refrain from defending any overarching good in the face of deep and persistent pluralism. Utilitarianism, a moral philosophy that gained prominence in the nineteenth century, provides an early and influential instantiation of a certain type of neutrality. Although utilitarianism openly endorses a distinct end, its appeal to neutrality lies in the fact that its end is universal and cognizant of human equality. John Stuart Mill, an English philosopher who helped lay the intellectual groundwork for both liberalism and utilitarianism, describes the latter theory as: “The creed which accepts as the foundation of morals, Utility, or the Greatest Happiness Principle, holds that actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to produce the reverse of happiness.”17 Mill here recognizes that utilitarianism pursues a specific good, clashing with the contemporary understanding of neutrality as prohibiting the state from promoting certain ends over others. But the utilitarian end of overall societal happiness is arguably neutral as it tends to transcend moral disagreement. Furthermore, the equal treatment all citizens receive under a utilitarian framework strengthens its claim to neutrality: “Utilitarians emphasize that agents are to be neutral in making their decisions. What this means is that under Utilitarianism, everyone counts for the same, and nobody counts for more than anybody else.”18 Thus, while not neutral in the broadest sense, utilitarianism’s equal consideration for all within a single, ostensibly noncontroversial moral framework makes it neutral in important respects. Utilitarianism served as America’s guiding moral philosophy throughout much of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, with its emphasis on impartiality finding expression in political rhetoric and policy. In a 1914 speech, for instance, President Woodrow Wilson



structural reform, Keynesian economics offered a way for government to control the economy without having to choose among controversial conceptions of the good society.”21 The circumstances of the 1940s made Keynesian neutrality all the more appealing. When World War II and the Holocaust exposed the world to the dangers of subordinating individual rights to collective goals, the fact that Keynesian economics did not promote a specific vision of society became particularly compelling. Nazi Germany and Imperial Japan’s perversion of republican concepts of the “common good” and “general will” to justify atrocities demonstrated how political systems that place collective interests above individual rights can become vehicles for oppression when divorced from liberal safeguards. In this historical context, Keynesian economics’ potential to facilitate government’s control of the economy without imposing specific moral ends made it ideologically palatable across the political spectrum. Of course, Keynesian economics did seek a distinct end, but this end could be sold to the American public because it was not politicized or controversial. Keynes articulated this end in The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money: “Consumption… is the sole end and object of all economic activity.”22 This remarkably simple goal allowed for government to influence economic output while fitting neatly within the liberal framework as it treated citizens as unencumbered selves capable of making their own choices and remained neutral towards competing conceptions of the good life. Thus, while Keynesianism offered stronger rights protections than utilitarianism, the two frameworks closely resembled each other with respect to their ends: both frameworks openly endorsed substantive ends that they cast as universal, nonsectarian, and therefore compatible with



liberal pluralism. Where utilitarianism identified happiness as the common metric through which actions could be ethically assessed, Keynesianism put forth consumption as an analogous metric for assessing economic policy. This substitution reflected Keynesianism’s status as an economic rather than ethical theory. Taken together, utilitarianism and Keynesianism resembled an intermediate stage in the development of liberal neutrality. In both cases, distinct ends were recognized as legitimate but these ends were conceivably neutral insofar as they were understood to be universal and impartial. The reasonably uncontroversial nature of these frameworks helped give them broad-based appeal, but it was the outcomes they yielded — not their commitment to neutrality as such — that ultimately defined their success. I discuss these theories to raise questions about the basis of our dominant contemporary understanding of neutrality. Setting arcane philosophical definitions of neutrality aside, is a state that refuses to recognize any societal good more “neutral” than one that recognizes happiness as its overarching good? What about a state that sets consumption as its end goal? I do not mean to suggest that either Keynesianism or utilitarianism is somehow more neutral than contemporary liberal frameworks, but I do think that it is worth reflecting on the potential arbitrariness of neutrality as a fixed concept. Why, in the wake of A Theory of Justice, was neutrality transformed from being a peripheral feature of liberal governance into a defining criterion of political legitimacy itself?



## The Emergence of Rawlsian Neutrality



When Rawls wrote A Theory of Justice in 1971, his motivation was to challenge the predominant belief framework of the time: utilitarianism. Rawls claimed that utilitarianism failed



to guarantee the basic rights and liberties of all citizens, treating individuals as mere instruments for maximizing societal welfare without recognizing their innate worth. He sought a conception of justice that would remedy this flaw. As he explains in the preface: “I do not believe that utilitarianism can provide a satisfactory account of the basic rights and liberties of citizens as free and equal persons, a requirement of absolutely first importance for an account of democratic institutions.”24 Rawls’s insistence on the priority of basic rights and liberties stemmed from his conviction that humans are born free and equal, with an inherent dignity that must be respected no matter what. This egalitarian commitment extended beyond rights to encompass social and economic outcomes. Rawls regarded many inequalities not as products of individual merit or failure, but as consequences of morally arbitrary factors — what he called the “natural lottery” of genetic endowment and the “social lottery” of family circumstances and social position. Because these factors are morally undeserved, Rawls argued that principles of justice must include mechanisms to mitigate the inequalities they produce. As he writes: “To treat all persons equally, to provide genuine equality of opportunity, society must give more attention to those with fewer native assets and to those born into the less favorable social positions. The idea is to redress the bias of contingencies in the direction of equality.”26 Utilitarianism’s inability to “give more attention” to the losers of the natural and social lotteries provides the rationale for Rawls’ second objection to the philosophy. As he explains: “A convincing account of basic rights and liberties, and of their priority, was the first objective of justice as fairness. A second objective was



to integrate that account with an understanding of democratic equality, which led to the principle of fair equality of opportunity and the difference principle.”27 These two objectives — protecting basic liberties and securing democratic equality — form the pillars of Rawlsian justice. Together, they express a commitment to treating citizens as free and equal, not only in their formal rights but in the social conditions under which those rights are exercised. This dual commitment to liberty and equality marks the fusion of Rawls’s liberal and egalitarian foundations. It was to provide a conceivably impartial justification for these commitments that Rawls introduced the original position. In the thought experiment, individuals choose principles of justice behind a veil of ignorance that obscures their own social status, natural talents, and personal beliefs. Because no one can design rules to advantage themselves without knowledge of who they will be upon entering society, the principles of justice derived from this vantage point are meant to be impartial. It is Rawls’s use of the veil of ignorance that most clearly connects his theory to the idea of liberal neutrality. Because the veil obscures individuals’ personal conceptions of the good life, the principles they select do not rely on any particular vision of the good. Rawls characterizes this setup as fair precisely because of the symmetry it establishes among persons: “given the circumstances of the original position, the symmetry of everyone’s relations to each other, this initial situation is fair between individuals as moral persons, that is, as rational beings with their own ends and capable, I shall assume, of a sense of justice.”29 A political order structured



according to principles chosen under these conditions would therefore remain neutral among competing conceptions of the good life. Yet for critics, it is precisely this construction that renders Rawlsian neutrality suspect. The fairness of the original position depends on substantive assumptions about persons as rational, morally independent agents with their own ends — assumptions that reflect a distinctly liberal moral outlook. By embedding these commitments into what is presented as an impartial procedure, Rawls is said to smuggle liberal-egalitarian values into the foundations of his theory, thereby undermining the neutrality it claims to secure. It is accordingly no coincidence that the principles of justice derived from the original position are liberal-egalitarian; they follow predictably from the assumptions built into Rawls’s starting point. In a later work, Rawls himself conceded that citizens must adhere to liberal-egalitarian principles of justice if the conception of justice as fairness put forth in Theory is to hold: “An essential feature of a well-ordered society associated with justice as fairness is that all its citizens endorse this conception on the basis of what I now call a comprehensive philosophical doctrine. They accept, as rooted in this doctrine, its two principles of justice.”31 The fact that justice as fairness relies upon citizens endorsing a single comprehensive doctrine — an all-encompassing system of beliefs and values that guides how individuals understand life, morality, and society — presents a major problem in the context of pluralistic democracies. In such societies, citizens inevitably adhere to a wide range of comprehensive doctrines, many of which rest on moral, religious, or philosophical commitments that diverge sharply from Rawls’s liberal-egalitarian assumptions. contestable conceptions of practical rationality and of justice,”37 ultimately denying the pluralism it claims to defend. Nonetheless, whatever its flaws or contradictions, A Theory of Justice quickly became immensely influential, ushering in a new philosophical paradigm. Within a few years, the Rawlsian framework had become an unavoidable point of reference for political theory. As libertarian philosopher Robert Nozick emphasized in 1974, philosophers “now must either work within Rawls’s theory, or explain why not.”38 Moreover, Rawls’ influence extended far beyond the narrow confines of academic political theory. As the 1970s wore on, a new generation of public figures — neoliberals — adapted Rawls’s ideas of liberal neutrality for their own purposes. They were less interested in Rawls’s own commitment to neutrality — he remained at his core a staunch egalitarian, critical of welfare-state capitalism — than in using the concept of neutrality to justify their own political and economic projects. Even as philosophers continued to engage with Rawls’s egalitarian ideals, politicians ignored his critiques of capitalism and overwhelmingly embraced neutrality alone. Detached from Rawls’s redistributive project, neutrality proved highly compatible with neoliberal arguments. The notion that individual freedom required state neutrality toward economic arrangements was used to justify pro-market policies and the inequalities they yielded. Although Rawls explicitly rejected such conclusions, neutrality provided a vocabulary through



which inegalitarian outcomes could be presented as natural products of fair procedures rather than as substantive political choices requiring moral justification. This appropriation was facilitated by the flexibility of Rawls’s framework. In seeking to construct a theory resilient to philosophical disagreement, Rawls supplied conceptual tools that could be repurposed in divergent ways. Though his theory contained robust resources for challenging free-market arrangements, “different parts of Rawls’s theory could be used to justify all sorts of political arrangements.”42 Furthermore, just as World War II concerns about totalitarianism helped facilitate the adoption of Keynsianism, Rawlsian neutrality proved especially amenable to neoliberal reinterpretation in the context of Cold War liberal anxieties about state power and collective economic control. Eventually, as neoliberalism became increasingly entrenched as America’s political economic framework, the concept of neutrality became untethered from Rawls, taking on a life of its own. With this shift, Rawls’s liberal-egalitarian vision — centered on correcting morally arbitrary inequalities and ensuring that free and equal citizens could exercise their rights under fair social conditions — was eclipsed by a thinner procedural ideal. What persisted was not justice as fairness, but a conception of neutrality that treated market outcomes as presumptively legitimate so long as formal rights were respected — an understanding that disregarded Rawls’s insistence that justice requires attention to the social and economic conditions under which rights are exercised, not merely their formal recognition. While neoliberalism succeeded in casting its policy prescriptions as neutral, the rationality used to justify those prescriptions was anything but, emerging from a specific



intellectual tradition with its own substantive views about freedom, authority, and the proper ends of political life. Neoliberalism’s reliance on tradition-specific rationality further reinforces MacIntyre’s claim that rationality can only exist within traditions that supply their own standards of reasoning and judgment. Although neoliberal ideas had circulated among intellectuals since the 1930s, they remained largely peripheral until the economic crises of the 1970s gave them political traction. Central to the neoliberal tradition was the Chicago School, a group of free-market economists who mounted a comprehensive attack on government intervention over decades. Particularly influential was Milton Friedman’s work. Writing at the height of Keynesian dominance, Friedman’s Capitalism and Freedom (1962) rejected state intervention in the economy on the grounds that economic freedom and political freedom were inseparable. Any attempt by the state to manage markets, he argued, necessarily infringed on individual liberty. This was not a neutral claim about efficiency alone, but a normative judgment rooted in a particular conception of freedom as non-interference. Friedman’s thinking drew heavily on the work of Friedrich Hayek, whose earlier The Road to Serfdom (1944) articulated neoliberalism’s more dramatic understandings. Hayek argued that even well-intentioned economic planning risked sliding toward totalitarianism, collapsing the distinction between Keynesian demand management and Soviet central planning into a difference of degree rather than kind. For Hayek, both involved coercive direction of economic life and



therefore posed a threat to individual liberty — an ideal he deemed to be “the highest political end.”45 



























Seen through MacIntyre’s lens, this intellectual lineage makes clear that neoliberal rationality is not a neutral mode of reasoning that stands above moral disagreement. It is the rationality of a tradition — one that privileges individual autonomy and market outcomes while regarding pursuit of shared ends with deep suspicion. Today, the fruits of neoliberal rationality are unmistakable. Neoliberalism’s lodestar of free-market economic growth has given rise to an economy governed by global market forces and large multinational corporations. By its own metrics, this economic order has been remarkably successful. The United States has outpaced other wealthy Western democracies in overall growth throughout the twenty-first century, making neoliberalism’s aggregate monetary merits difficult to dismiss.46 















But this success is inextricably linked with what neoliberal rationality chooses to measure. Growth has been unevenly distributed, with productivity gains flowing disproportionately to capital rather than labor. Whole regions have been hollowed out by offshoring and consolidation, wages have stagnated despite rising output, and economic security has eroded for large segments of the population. In privileging efficiency and aggregate performance over shared prosperity and worker dignity, neoliberalism has produced an economy that excels on paper while leaving many citizens materially and socially worse off than the headline numbers suggest.



The neoliberal transformation of the U.S. economy has led many Americans to feel frustrated with the market-dictated socioeconomic order that promised prosperity but instead delivered social fragmentation and shrinking opportunity. Cognizant of this discontent, a growing number of intellectuals are attacking neutrality for the practical consequences it yields rather than for its philosophical foundations. For these critics, the liberal emphasis on procedural neutrality has enabled monopolistic corporations to amass power beyond the reach of democratic institutions, rendering individuals detached from the economic arrangements that govern their lives. As this argument goes, by declining to make substantive moral judgments about what a good or just economy should look like, liberal neutrality has helped legitimize an economic order in which workers are rendered dislocated and vulnerable to the arbitrary authority of large firms. Employment relations that once would have been identified as cases of domination are recast as private choices insulated from political scrutiny. As political philosopher Patrick Deneen laments: “all but forgotten are arguments, such as those made in the early Republic, that liberty consists of independence from the arbitrariness not only of a king but of an employer.”47 Criticisms of liberal neutrality do not stop at the economic arrangements it is said to yield. Critics also argue that neutrality has contributed to broader forms of social fragmentation by hollowing out shared norms, civic obligations, and institutions that once bound citizens together. The resulting society is defined by widespread loneliness, declining civic participation, and a weakened sense of collective responsibility — social pathologies that are incredibly difficult to address using the toolkit of contemporary liberal neutrality.

Taken together, these economic and social criticisms form the thrust of the contemporary attack against the practical consequences of liberal neutrality. Crucially though, these criticisms come into tension with another major criticism of liberal neutrality with roots in the immediate aftermath of A Theory of Justice: that liberal neutrality — at least as devised through the original position — is impossible. Critics such as MacIntyre did not argue that neutrality produced bad outcomes; they argued that it never truly existed. Under this view, Rawls’s purportedly impartial framework already presupposed a distinctively liberal moral anthropology, smuggling substantive commitments about autonomy, rationality, and moral independence into the foundations of justice. The problem with these two charges coexisting — one focused on the harms allegedly produced by liberal neutrality in practice, the other on its theoretical incoherence — is that they pull in opposite directions and cannot be sustained simultaneously. For present societal ills to be attributed to liberal neutrality, liberal neutrality must not only be possible but actively governing the socioeconomic structures by which we live. Conversely, if liberal neutrality is an impossibility, it cannot plausibly be held responsible for current discontent. The most logical conclusion to draw from this apparent paradox is one I have alluded to throughout this paper: today’s societal ills which are increasingly attributed to liberal neutrality are not actually the results of neutrality as derived through the original position but of a different construal of “neutrality” that bears little philosophical resemblance to the Rawlsian conception. This clarification is not merely pedantic. When philosophical theories are brought from the theoretical realm into the realm of real-world politics, nuances are inevitably obscured, and over time, popular understandings can devolve into little more than caricatures of the initial ideas. In



Rawls’s case, neutrality as a by-product of his project to defend liberal-egalitarian principles of justice turned into a political slogan used to shield economic arrangements from moral and democratic scrutiny. What is criticized today, then, is not Rawlsian neutrality, but a procedural, market-friendly substitute that invokes neutrality to foreclose contestation rather than to ensure justice.



## Conclusion: What Now?



At a time when political polarization is rampant and trust in key American institutions is at historic lows, it is tempting to lean especially heavily on procedural neutrality. If Americans are deeply divided over major moral and practical issues, it seems only logical that government should now more than ever refrain from pushing its idea of the good society on citizens. Even if it were to try to do so, it would be impossible. Widespread public distrust of institutions would surely stand in its way. These arguments against government taking a more active role in promoting the general welfare are compelling. But to infer that the societal divisions and ills the United States currently face call for us to double down on neutrality is exactly the wrong conclusion to draw. In recent decades, neutrality — at least as it has been understood for the past fifty years — has not brought Americans together by fostering tolerance and respect for one another’s differences as the founders hoped it might. Instead, the undying emphasis on neutrality that has defined 21st century life in the United States has left us lacking any common projects, ideals, or values. Even as technology has enabled Americans to connect across thousands of miles, bridging once-insurmountable geographic distances, Americans today may have a harder time explaining what binds them to their fellow citizens — and to the nation itself — than ever before. Neutrality



is not the sole cause of the country’s deep disunity, but its elevation of procedure over substance has certainly exacerbated the country’s civic emptiness. Thus, what the United States needs now is not better refereeing of disagreement, but a renewed basis for unity. This is not a new insight. American political theorists and leaders alike have long understood the importance of active, cultivated attachment to the political community amid moral and ideological pluralism. In his Farewell Address, Washington urged Americans to recognize that their collective future depended on their dedication to social cohesion: It is of infinite moment that you should properly estimate the immense value of your national Union to your collective and individual happiness; that you should cherish a cordial, habitual, and immovable attachment to it; accustoming yourselves to think and speak of it as of the palladium of your political safety and prosperity; watching for its preservation with jealous anxiety; discountenancing whatever may suggest even a suspicion that it can in any event be abandoned, and indignantly frowning upon the first dawning of every attempt to alienate any portion of our country from the rest, or to enfeeble the sacred ties which now link together the various parts. 



In the second half of the twentieth century, we as a nation lost sight of how important



unity is to our shared happiness. During World War II, collective mobilization and sacrifice brought the country together, temporarily muting the civic anxieties that accompany pluralism. And in subsequent decades, broad-based postwar prosperity reinforced the feeling that Americans were part of a common project. By the 1970s and 80s, however, the conditions that had once sustained national cohesion no longer held. Unity came to be treated as an inheritance rather than as something requiring active cultivation, even as society grew more fragmented by the year.

proved increasingly incapable of fostering unity. What we need now is not merely a better or truer neutrality. We need to engage more deeply with the questions that our longstanding devotion to liberal neutrality has prevented from entering the public discourse. We need to recommit ourselves to doing the hard work required to develop unity in a liberal democracy. This can take multiple forms. 



For Rawls, unity is achieved through shared principles of justice that regulate cooperation



among free and equal citizens, even amid deep disagreement about the good life. Sandel, by contrast, argues that unity requires shared civic purposes and common ends, contending that procedural agreement alone cannot bind citizens together. Which of these paths offers a more durable basis for unity remains an open question. But what is clear is that neutrality alone cannot do the work we now ask of it. The future of liberal democracy depends on rediscovering what, beyond procedure, binds citizens to one another and to the political community they share.
